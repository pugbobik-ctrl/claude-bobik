# References: freestanding flat die-cut place card ("Dinner", Colorblock x DNA Kitchen)

Method note. "Opened" = I fetched the page and read the statement used. Several museum sites (moma.org, brooklynmuseum.org, dezeen.com, artic.edu) returned 403 to my fetcher; where that happened I say so and cite a source I did open instead.
Honest gap: I could NOT find or confirm any contemporary graphic-studio die-cut place card / table piece (the search tool returned only stationery shops and Behance listings). The list below is therefore artists, designers, paper engineers and researchers, not studio case studies. See "Gaps" at the end.

## A. SLOT / INTERLOCK (flat planes crossing each other)

1. **Naum Gabo, Constructed Head No. 1 (1915) and No. 2 (1916; enlarged 1964)**
 - Mechanism: planes of card, wood or steel intersect and interlock; each plane braces the one it crosses, so the head stands with no solid mass. Tate: "interlocking sheets of card, wood and steel". Staedel records No. 1 as three-layered plywood, 53.5 x 45 x 34.5 cm (cardboard maquettes are mentioned in secondary sources; I did not confirm which version is which).
 - Why relevant: the closest art-historical parent of the "circle-over-half-circle standing on a cross-slot" mood example. A portrait made only of crossing profiles gives you a place card whose silhouette changes as you walk around the table.
 - Sources (opened): https://sammlung.staedelmuseum.de/en/work/constructed-head-no-1-1 ; https://www.tate.org.uk/whats-on/tate-st-ives/naum-gabo/naum-gabo

2. **Nathalie Du Pasquier, painted wooden "constructed things" and painted-paper cut-outs (exhibition "Big Objects Not Always Silent", Kunsthalle Wien, 2016)**
 - Mechanism: simple geometric volumes and flat cut-out shapes built as tabletop-scale objects, arranged as still lifes. In her own booklet text she describes works that "combine 3D pieces with a kind of trompe-l'oeil painted on paper and cut out", so you can't tell what is 3D and what is flat.
 - Why relevant: a colour-block, poster-flat vocabulary of pebbles, arches and discs that already reads as a table still life. The flat/solid ambiguity is a usable idea for a card.
 - Sources (opened; PDF, text extracted): https://kunsthallewien.at/assets/exhibitions/2016-07-nathalie-du-pasquier-big-objects-not-always-silent/Nathalie-Du-Pasquier-Booklet_EN.pdf . Slot/notch construction is NOT stated in the text, so treat it as inspiration, not a mechanism precedent.

## B. SINGLE-SHEET CUT-AND-LIFT / SELF-FORMING FROM ONE SHEET

3. **Josef Albers, Vorkurs paper studies, Bauhaus Dessau, 1927-28 (photographed by Erich Consemueller); continued at Black Mountain College**
 - Mechanism: students got a sheet and one rule, cut and fold with no waste. Known results: saddle (concentric circle creases that twist into a curve), hyperbolic paraboloid, zigzag corrugation, "puff ball" (three interlinked circles), dome-shaped cut-fold "cobweb valentines". Bauhaus-imaginista: "the folded angles give this piece surprising rigidity".
 - Why relevant: the exact methodology the brief names. The no-waste rule produces a stand that is the card itself.
 - Sources (opened): http://www.bauhaus-imaginista.org/articles/1703/open-your-eyes/ ; https://origamiheaven.com/historyjosephalbers.htm ; CCA record of a Consemueller print of a paper exercise: https://www.cca.qc.ca/en/search/details/collection/object/3260

4. **Erik and Martin Demaine, curved-crease sculptures (Computational Origami, in MoMA collection 2008; Renwick Series 2011, SAAM)**
 - Mechanism: a single sheet creased along curves settles into a self-supporting equilibrium form. Their site: "paper shapes itself into a natural equilibrium form". Their history page traces it back to Albers's concentric-circle model.
 - Why relevant: a researcher's version of Albers. A printed disc with scored concentric arcs, die-cut from one sheet, would stand as a soft saddle or bowl without any second piece. That also answers "must not look like lettering".
 - Sources (opened): https://erikdemaine.org/curved/ ; https://erikdemaine.org/curved/history/

5. **Aleksandr Rodchenko, Hanging Spatial Constructions, Moscow, 1920-21 (No. 12 is the surviving one)**
 - Mechanism: concentric cuts into one flat sheet (plywood) that is then opened into an expanding 3D lattice. Tate Papers: "created by cutting into a plane sheet and then wiring them together at the swivel points". MoMA's catalogue text (from a search result; the page itself returned 403 to me) says it hangs and turns in the air currents. UNVERIFIED by my own fetch of MoMA.
 - Why relevant: Moscow-native and Constructivist, and it is a single-disc cut-and-open, a very different object from a folded tent card. Sit it on a short wire stem or ring (mood example 2) and it becomes a standing spiral.
 - Source (opened): https://www.tate.org.uk/research/tate-papers/12/aleksandr-rodchenkos-lines-of-force . Unopened (403): https://www.moma.org/collection/works/81043

6. **Yuken Teruya, Notice-Forest series (e.g. "Notice - Forest (Autumn)", 2002, McDonald's paper bag; Smithsonian American Art Museum)**
 - Mechanism: a tree silhouette is cut into one face of a bag, then the cut piece is bent inward to "take root" with a drop of glue. Nothing is added or removed. The hole left behind is the other half of the image.
 - Why relevant: the "cut shape becomes its own foot" idea. Close in spirit to Peter Callesen (already on your list), but more graphic and with an everyday-object logic, and the negative hole can carry the guest's name.
 - Sources (opened): https://americanart.si.edu/artwork/notice-forest-autumn-109656 ; https://www.sugarhillmuseum.org/yuken-teruya

## C. FOLD-OPEN / FLAT TO VOLUME (ships flat, expands, is held by its own geometry)

7. **Tokujin Yoshioka, Honey-Pop armchair, 2001**
 - Mechanism: 120 layers of glassine honeycomb paper, glued flat, that peel open accordion-style into a volume; the final shape is set by the first person sitting. No frame. Cooper Hewitt.
 - Why relevant: a design-world proof that a paper honeycomb stands and bears weight. A tiny honeycomb "pebble" that a guest pulls open at the table gives a reveal moment with no assembly instructions.
 - Source (opened): http://www.cooperhewitt.org/2014/02/07/a-precious-posterior-preserved-for-posterity/ (AIC and MoMA pages 403 to me).

8. **Issey Miyake Reality Lab with Jun Mitani, 132 5. (shown Galerie Kreo, Paris, 7 Sept 2010)**
 - Mechanism: one piece of cloth, folded flat to geometric stars and swirls by computer-generated origami patterns, that opens into angular volumes. Design Observer.
 - Why relevant: research-driven (software-generated crease patterns), and the visual logic is flat geometric shapes that become volume. The flat-pack star or swirl as a card is an unusual form for a table.
 - Source (opened): https://designobserver.com/132-5-issey-miyake/

9. **Matt Shlian, paper engineer (Cranbrook MFA 2006; collaborations with University of Michigan engineers on nanoscale folding)**
 - Mechanism: precise tessellation folds, no glue or cuts in the folded work; sculpture stands or hangs by tension in the folds. "I begin with a system of folding and at a particular moment the material takes over."
 - Why relevant: an artist who is also commissioned for commercial paper pieces and publishes with scientists, which fits "research-oriented". One monochrome folded tessellation could be the "pebble".
 - Source (opened): https://mymodernmet.com/matt-shlian-paper-engineering/

10. **Marion Bataille, ABC3D (2008)**
 - Mechanism: a pop-up alphabet in black, cream and red using fold-ups, fold-outs, pull-tabs and undercuts; each letter stands up by a different hinge logic.
 - Why relevant: a lexicon of small pop-up hinges in a 3-colour palette. CAUTION: it is letterforms, which risks echoing the poster. Use the hinge ideas, not the alphabet.
 - Source (opened): https://seattlereviewofbooks.com/reviews/alpha-beta

11. **Katsumi Komagata / One Stroke, Little Tree (2008), A Cloud (2007), Ichigu (2015)**
 - Mechanism: Little Tree uses pop-ups and a die-cut tree shape on varied card stock; A Cloud uses cloud-shaped die cuts through different papers; Ichigu is eight 4-panel cards in a box, printed black on one side and white on the other, whose "landscapes change with the light and from one juxtaposition to another". How the Ichigu panels stand is NOT stated on the page; UNVERIFIED.
 - Why relevant: a paper-first Japanese practice built on stock, die-cut and shadow instead of illustration. The black/white double-sided 4-panel card is a candidate model for a two-sided standing place card.
 - Source (opened): https://books-on-books.com/2020/03/22/books-on-books-collection-katsumi-komagata/

12. **Kveta Pacovska, object books and paper sculptures (paper and metal objects 1960s-70s; "Unfold Enfold", 2004)**
 - Mechanism: Unfold/Enfold is "one continuous folded page" with die-cut pages; her 1960s-70s paper and metal sculptures were shown at GHMP Prague, 2019-20 ("Run till the End"). Whether any early paper sculptures free-stand is not stated; UNVERIFIED.
 - Why relevant: an Eastern-European tradition of treating the colour-and-cut page as sculpture; a good palette and "cut as drawing" reference.
 - Sources (opened): https://library.louisville.edu/artists-books/unfold-enfold ; https://www.ghmp.cz/en/exhibitions/kveta-pacovska-run-till-the-end/

## D. HINGE / HOLDER / TWO-PIECE (the base is part of the idea)

13. **Lygia Clark, Bichos (from 1960)**
 - Mechanism: polished aluminium plates inter-articulated by hinges ("spines"); the owner folds and reconfigures them and they stand in many shapes. SFMOMA Open Space.
 - Why relevant: a standing flat-plate form that the guest can re-pose, so each setting is different. Paper cannot hold a metal hinge, but a die-cut paper "living hinge" or a folded fabric/tape joint can imitate it.
 - Source (opened): https://openspace.sfmoma.org/2020/05/essential-workings/

14. **Constantin Brancusi, sculpture and base as a pair (studio collection: 137 sculptures, 87 bases; five bases shown alone, Brummer Gallery, 1926)**
 - Mechanism: sculpture rests on stacked limestone/oak bases that are "not merely a support; an integral element"; he swapped bases between sculptures daily. Centre Pompidou.
 - Why relevant: the philosophy behind your mood example 1 (shape over a second piece). A set of 3-4 interchangeable bases (a ring, a slotted block, a wire cradle) and a set of bodies: any body on any base gives each guest a different pairing.
 - Source (opened): https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/collection/brancusis-studio

## E. WEIGHT / BALANCE / FRICTION (no joint at all)

15. **Richard Serra, One Ton Prop (House of Cards) (1969) and Prop pieces (1968)**
 - Mechanism: four 48 x 48 in lead plates, each leaning on an edge against the next, "no attachments or welding". Guggenheim Bilbao: "free-standing sculptures without permanent anchors or welding... lead plates lean against each other without adhesives or fasteners".
 - Why relevant: the leaning-plane version of a card house, with a different logic from the Eames House of Cards (slotted cards) that you already have: friction and a counter-weight with no slots at all. A 3-card lean, cut to be only stable at one angle, would be a place setting that is also a tiny sculpture. NAME CLASH: do not call it "house of cards".
 - Source (opened): https://guggenheim-bilbao.eus/en/learn/schools/teachers-guides/weight-and-balance . (MoMA page appeared in results; not opened.)

16. **Peter Fischli and David Weiss, Quiet Afternoon (Stiller Nachmittag) / Equilibres, 1984-85**
 - Mechanism: household and kitchen objects balanced at the edge of falling, then photographed. Tate: "precariously balanced household utensils on the verge of collapse".
 - Why relevant: balance and a pause at the table; the equilibrium is the content. A card whose centre of gravity is deliberately off, so it stands only on the right foot.
 - Source (opened): https://tate.org.uk/whats-on/tate-modern/fischli-weiss

## F. COLLECTIVE WHOLE / THE TABLE AS MEDIUM

17. **Lucy + Jorge Orta, 70 x 7 The Meal (series since 2000; Act X, Napa, 2002)**
 - Mechanism: each seat has its own printed plate; one custom-printed fabric runner ties all seats into one band of colour. Seven guests each invite seven more.
 - Why relevant: an artists' dinner where an object marks each place and the whole table adds up to a single piece. CAUTION: you already have receipt-paper runners, so the lesson is the plate-level individual marker, not another runner.
 - Source (opened): https://studio-orta.com/en/artwork/64/70-x-7-the-meal-act-x-napa-miu-v

18. **Judy Chicago, The Dinner Party (1974-79)**
 - Mechanism: 39 place settings on a 48-ft-per-side triangular table; each place has its own runner embroidered with the woman's name, a painted plate and sculptural elements, all standing on a floor of 998 further names.
 - Why relevant: the canonical example of a place setting as an individual portrait (shape, colour, name) within a set. Brooklyn Museum page returned 403; details are from Wikipedia (opened), so treat it as secondary.
 - Source (opened): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dinner_Party

## Five mechanisms least used in event stationery and most promising
1. **Body + interchangeable base (the base is part of the idea)**: Brancusi, Gabo, Du Pasquier. Two or three flat parts per guest where the second part is a notched ring, block or wire cradle; sets of bodies and bases can be re-paired. Most direct fit with mood 1 and mood 2.
2. **Self-forming crease from one sheet (saddle, concentric-circle, curved crease)**: Albers, Demaine. A printed disc scored with a few arcs stands as a soft bowl or saddle by tension alone. Zero assembly, no slots, a form nobody will confuse with a place-card tent.
3. **Concentric cut-and-open spiral**: Rodchenko, with the wire/ring holder from mood 2. A single die-cut disc that opens to a coil; sits on a stem or ring.
4. **Cut-shape-becomes-its-own-foot (fold the silhouette from inside the sheet)**: Teruya, Callesen. The shape and its stand come from one sheet; the cut hole carries the name. Matches the grape-cluster tab idea, but more minimal.
5. **Lean / friction / off-centre balance with no joints**: Serra, Fischli & Weiss. Two or three cut shapes that only stand in one arrangement; the guest has to place them. Avoid calling it "house of cards" because of Eames.
Runners-up: flat-to-volume honeycomb or star (Yoshioka, Miyake) for a reveal; individual-plate-plus-whole-table (Orta, Chicago) if the table as a whole is to read as one composition.
